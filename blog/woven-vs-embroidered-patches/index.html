Woven vs Embroidered Patches: Which Should You Order?

By the SAIM Label factory team · Updated 2026-09-21 · 10 min read

Short answer: the difference is construction. A woven patch has your artwork woven into the fabric as interlaced yarn, so small text and thin lines stay sharp. An embroidered patch has thread stitched on top of a twill base, so it has raised, tactile texture but small letters close up. Choose woven when detail matters more than texture, embroidery when texture is the point. At B2B volumes the prices overlap; the real decision is detail versus texture.

The construction difference

A woven patch is made like fabric: colored polyester yarns interlace on a computer loom, and your artwork is the weave. An embroidered patch starts with a base fabric (twill, usually) and a machine stitches thread on top of it. Everything else — edges, backings, shapes — follows from that one difference.

Because woven yarn sits flat, a woven patch can hold letters just a few millimeters tall. Because embroidery thread has physical thickness, small type closes up — but that same thickness is what gives embroidery its raised, premium hand feel.

When woven wins

  • Logos with small text — brand names, URLs, slogans under the mark.
  • Complex crests — multi-element artwork that embroidery would blur.
  • Hat front panels where fine lettering must stay clean at 6–8 cm wide.
  • Lightweight garments — the patch should not deform thin fabric.

When embroidery wins

  • Bold, simple logos — letters, mascots, shields with strong outlines.
  • Uniforms and clubs — the raised badge look reads as official.
  • Caps needing 3D puff — foam-backed embroidery pops on curved panels.
  • Varsity programs — chenille-style lettering for jackets and hoodies.

Where each one hits its limit

Both constructions have a point where the artwork stops working, and knowing it before you send files saves a revision cycle.

  • Woven, at the fine end. Woven holds lettering and hairlines that embroidery cannot, because there is no thread height to close the gaps. Where it runs out is soft colour: a photograph or a smooth gradient has to be simplified into solid colour blocks, and every extra colour is a separate weaving decision rather than a free choice.
  • Embroidered, at the small end. Thread has physical thickness. Lettering has a minimum size before the stitches merge together, and very fine lines are usually thickened or dropped when the artwork is digitised. Bold shapes, heavy outlines and solid blocks of colour are what embroidery does best.
  • Both, at the edge. A merrow border prefers a regular shape. Irregular silhouettes switch to a laser-cut or heat-cut edge instead, which is a fine result but a different look — worth knowing before you approve a die-cut outline.

If your artwork has two personalities — a detailed wordmark plus a bold outer shape — that is usually a sign to look at the hybrid construction below rather than forcing one technique to do both jobs.

The practical consequence: detailed artwork usually costs you nothing extra in the weave, so a complex logo is often better value made woven. Embroidery closes the gap on small, simple, low-stitch designs — and loses it again as coverage grows, because every extra stitch is priced.

Two production notes that move the final number: iron-on backing should always be heat-tested on your actual garment fabric before bulk, and merrow borders prefer regular shapes — irregular silhouettes get laser-cut edges instead.

If you are still split, the tiebreaker is almost always the smallest element in your logo. A web address, a registration line or a fine ring of text decides it for you: if those have to be readable, order woven.

The hybrid option: woven centre, embroidered border

Plenty of real orders do not choose. A common construction pairs a woven centre panel — which carries the fine lettering — with an embroidered border or outer shape. The badge keeps its raised, tactile edge where the texture is obvious and stays sharp where the text is.

It is usually the right answer when your artwork has two personalities: a detailed wordmark or crest in the middle, and a bold outer shape that wants to read as a badge. It costs more than either single construction because two processes run, but it is often the only way to get both the fine detail and the raised look in one patch.

Chenille follows the same logic: loop-pile letters for the main shape, with embroidered outlines and details stitched onto the felt base.

Wash and wear

Both constructions are built to survive normal garment life, and the same edge and backing menu is available for either. The differences show up at the extremes.

  • Fine detail under abrasion. Woven keeps its detail longer in high-wear positions, because the colour is the fabric rather than stitching sitting on top of it.
  • Surface scuffing. Embroidery hides surface wear better — the raised texture means light scuffing reads as part of the pattern.
  • Repeated laundering. Sewn-on patches of either construction handle repeated washing. Iron-on is the variable: performance depends on the adhesive and the garment fibre, so heat-test on an offcut before bulk, especially on stretch, coated or synthetic fabrics.
  • Weight on light fabric. Dense embroidery coverage makes a patch stiffer and heavier, which can pull on thin or drapey fabric. Woven sits flatter.

How to brief both in one RFQ

A comparison only means something if both quotes are built from identical information. Send the same files and the same specification, and ask for both constructions priced against each other.

What to include: vector artwork (AI, EPS, PDF or SVG) or a high-resolution image at finished size; the finished width and height in millimetres or inches; quantity per design; preferred edge and backing; and where the patch is going. If your logo contains small text, say what has to stay readable — that one detail usually settles the construction for you.

Custom samples are arranged together with the quote so artwork, construction and backing are confirmed before production. Sample cost and shipping depend on the product and are confirmed in the quote.

FAQ

Which is cheaper, woven or embroidered patches?

At typical B2B quantities the prices are close. Embroidery cost rises with stitch count and coverage; woven cost is driven more by size and quantity. Complex, detailed artwork is usually cheaper as a woven patch.

Which lasts longer on garments?

Both survive normal garment life when sewn on. For high-abrasion positions, woven patches keep detail longer; embroidery hides surface wear better because of its texture.

Can I combine woven and embroidered elements?

Yes. A common route is an embroidered badge with a woven center panel for small text, or matching woven labels with embroidered patches in one order.

What should I send to compare both options?

Send the same artwork, finished size and quantity, and ask for both constructions in one RFQ. SAIM Label produces both in-house, so the quote can compare them directly.

What is the difference between woven and embroidered patches?

Construction. A woven patch weaves your artwork into the fabric as interlaced yarn, so fine detail stays sharp. An embroidered patch stitches thread on top of a twill base, which gives raised texture but fills in small letters. Edges, backings and shapes follow the same menu for both.

Is embroidered or woven better for hats?

For a hat front panel, woven usually reads better: the panel is small and often carries a wordmark, and fine lettering stays legible. Choose embroidery with 3D puff when you want the logo to stand up off the cap instead of sitting flat.

Are embroidered patches more expensive than woven ones?

At B2B volumes the ranges overlap and both run on the same minimum, but embroidery is priced by stitch count and coverage, so dense designs climb faster. Woven is priced by size and quantity and barely reacts to artwork complexity, which is why detailed logos are often better value woven.

Do woven and embroidered patches have the same minimum order?

Yes. Both run on the same 50 pcs per design minimum and the same 10-12 day production window, so the construction you choose does not change your MOQ or your lead time.
